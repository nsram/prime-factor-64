Prime Factor 64 began with Sriram’s wish to memorize a small multiplication table with enough confidence that he would stop second-guessing himself. The conversation became a one-page game for practicing 64 selected multiplication facts. Sriram supplied the learning goal, tested the game, and made choices about difficulty and play. ChatGPT worked out the arithmetic, translated those choices into a playable design, and revised it against his feedback.

Two squares that needed a cue

The last two squares were the ones he hesitated on. He already had a cue for 19² = 361, one more than the 360 degrees in a full circle. For 17² = 289, ChatGPT offered the cue “17 is two numbers: 8 and 9.” Since 8 + 9 = 17, thinking “two, eight, nine” supplies the three digits.

Sriram then narrowed the goal to results greater than 100. He wanted the association to work in both directions, so 13 × 17 should bring 221 to mind and 221 should bring 13 × 17 to mind. He first asked for the prime-by-prime results over 100, then set the squares aside as the easier cases for this round of practice.

After the narrative come the UI history, naming discussion, alternatives, complete deck, and technical version log.

From 39 products to 55 results

Sriram proposed a difficulty order. Start with distinct primes, move to a prime times an odd composite below 20, then include a prime times an even composite. When he asked how many products this made, he also specified that 10 should be excluded. That removed 110, 130, 170, and 190 from these tiers.

The eight distinct-prime results are 119, 133, 143, 187, 209, 221, 247, and 323.

A game for the practice list

For those 39 products, Sriram asked for a single-page matching game that would finish once every expression had been paired with its result. He added a stopwatch and asked for each product to be displayed in one of two randomly chosen orders, such as 13 × 17 or 17 × 13.

More difficult products and repeated results

Asked what else could be troublesome, ChatGPT suggested six products of different even composites. The shortlist was 12 × 14 = 168, 12 × 16 = 192, 12 × 18 = 216, 14 × 16 = 224, 14 × 18 = 252, and 16 × 18 = 288. Those six would have brought the list to 45.

Sriram pointed out that two expressions with the same answer could not both appear in one game. Enumerating every qualifying composite-by-composite expression adds 20 expressions but only 16 new results. In the full pool, 59 unordered expressions therefore become 55 distinct results. The game keeps both factor pairs as options for each collision and deals only one expression for that result in a run.

Playing the first game

When Sriram asked how to fit 55 results, the design put them across seven boards, six with eight matches and one with seven. He approved implementation. Prime Match 55 used separate, shuffled columns for expressions and results. The player could start on either side, see the stopwatch and progress, and reach a score and review screen after the final match. Each new deal could change the card order, factor order, and any eligible alternate factor pair.

The last digit gave too much away

“There is a cheat. if the last digit of the result is unique in a set then its easier”

The player could work out just the units digit and scan for the sole candidate. The first fix grouped results that shared an ending before dealing boards, keeping at least two results for every ending used on those original seven boards. That preserved shuffling while making a last-digit shortcut insufficient on its own.

Pausing and giving the game a voice

Sriram asked for Pause and Resume to freeze the game. The board was hidden while paused, input was blocked, and the timer and any pending board advance resumed from where they had stopped. He then asked what sounds might increase engagement and approved adding them. The game gained a correct-match tone, a low miss tone, a board-clear chime, and a short completion cue, with a Sound toggle.

From recognizing a target to recalling it

Sriram liked the sounds and noticed a deeper issue while playing. He would make a crude estimate and look for a nearby target in the results column. He wanted something within the board cycle that would help the exact association stick. ChatGPT proposed two checks after each board, one asking for a product and one asking for the factors.

Sriram approved the idea and asked for a toggle and an early exit that still returned a score. The implemented checks hide the choices and prioritize missed or slower pairs. Factor recall accepts either order and either eligible factorization. Show answer reveals the equation without adding a scored attempt.

End game shows progress, elapsed time, and first-try accuracy on pairs attempted so far, plus recall accuracy when applicable and a review of missed pairs. A wrong guess is charged to the pair the player started from.

Sriram asked why the stopwatch stopped during recall. ChatGPT explained that the stopwatch measured time on the matching boards, while recall was scored for accuracy and allowed time to think or type. The game kept recall untimed.

Board sizes and the move to 60

What the player needed to see

On an eight-pair board, Sriram noted that solving seven made the last answer obvious. He decided that was acceptable and proposed a better use of attention. Completed cards moved to the top of both columns, visibly grouped and locked, leaving the unfinished cards together below.

Sriram asked how to copy the HTML so he could use the game source. The project kept a standalone HTML file alongside the hosted game. He also asked what the X at the upper left meant and said it was confusing. ChatGPT replaced it with a four-tile mark.

The four pair layout had one board too many

The game originally showed eight pairs per screen. Sriram asked for four and twelve as well, with a board count that followed the size dropdown. Preserving shared endings in the first four-pair deal made 15 boards. Sriram expected 14 and suggested allocating the final board first, then dealing the rest.

ChatGPT reserved a final triple first. Of the 13 full boards, 12 used two same-ending pairs and one used a same-ending triple plus a singleton. That single exception let the deck fit without an extra board. When the count next to the dropdown still appeared static to Sriram, each option was labeled with its own size and board count.

Five squares solved a layout problem

Sriram suggested adding 11², 13², 15², 17², and 19². Their results, 121, 169, 225, 289, and 361, brought the deck to 60. He revisited the first 15-board arrangement. This time all 15 boards could hold four pairs. Thirteen boards used two shared-ending pairs and two used a triple and a singleton. The twelve-pair option also divided evenly into five boards. Sriram felt 60 covered most of the tricky three-digit results he wanted to practice.

The final deck and session controls

Home and recall settings

Sriram asked for the top-left logo to return to the original start screen and for a due-diligence pass across the current spec. ChatGPT made the logo a Home link and had it stop the active timer and pending advance when leaving a game. Sriram then asked for an option of four recall checks and a different sound for each correct answer. The two-check toggle became an Off, 2, or 4 menu. Correct recall answers received different rising two-note chimes by prompt position.

From Prime Match to Prime Factor 64

Sriram compared Prime Match, Factor Match, Multiply Match, and Prime Factor for distinctiveness. He also revisited whether the 60 facts covered the right ground. He found 14² and 16² much more familiar than 18², considered a swap involving 15², then proposed keeping 15² and adding all three. The new squares were 14² = 196, 16² = 256, and 18² = 324. That left 63 results.

The last addition was 17 × 21 = 357. With 64 results, a full game could use equally sized boards of 4, 8, or 16 pairs. Sriram liked the sound of Prime Factor 64, approved the change, and kept four pairs as the default. The name carried forward the original prime-table idea even though the deck had grown to include composite factors.

The final deck contains the 55 earlier distinct results, eight specified squares, and 357. It is a targeted set of three-digit facts. For example, 144 can appear as 8 × 18 or 9 × 16. It was already present before the squares were added, and 12 × 12 was never added as a display option.

Make the length a separate choice

Sriram asked to choose the number of boards independently of the number of pairs per board. ChatGPT tied the menus to four session totals so changing the board size recalculates the board count while preserving the chosen number of matches.

Polishing the original release

Make factor feedback explain the guess

Sriram checked whether recall had sound. ChatGPT verified that correct answers play their own position-based chimes with Sound on, an incorrect answer plays the low cue, and Show answer is silent. Sriram then asked the game to multiply the two numbers he entered so he could see what his factors implied. He also asked the home page to state the factor limit. It now says every factor is 19 or less, except for 21 in 21 × 17.

Correct factor submissions now show the entered equation in the player’s order. For a miss, the app shows both the entered product and the target equation.

Give the finish its own sound

Sriram reported that the completion sound was missing in a four-board game and asked for a spectacular one. The existing cue was a brief three-note arpeggio close in character to the board-clear chime. ChatGPT replaced it with six rising notes, a sustained C-major chord, and a high flourish. The completion screen now triggers the fanfare from one shared path, including after audio resumes if the browser suspended it. With recall enabled, it plays when the player selects See score.

ChatGPT played the default four-board game to 16/16 in the browser. Programmatic checks covered the cue with recall off, with two checks, and with four, plus the 64-match finish, audio resumption, mute, and early exit. The original device-specific cause of the missing sound remained unconfirmed.

Two lines for factor feedback

Sriram asked for the two sentences to appear on separate lines. The tested feedback reads as follows.

Your factors give 13 × 18 = 234.
The match is 13 × 17 = 221.

What each collaborator contributed

Sriram was the learner, product designer, and playtester. His observations supplied requirements that only surfaced when the game was in use. ChatGPT made the proposals testable by enumerating the arithmetic, designing the board deals, and turning feedback into revised versions. The same process handled changes at very different scales, from the number of facts in the deck to the line break that made one answer easier to read.

Practice across sessions

Muse’s review pointed to a gap between sessions. The game showed which facts needed work, and Sriram approved a follow-up that would bring those facts back on a later visit.

Practice weak facts offers up to eight untimed prompts. A missed match can return as a product question, while a missed recall question returns in its original direction. Two unaided correct answers on different days retire a prompt from the weak list. For example, the app tracks 6 × 18 and 9 × 12 separately when asking for the product. When it asks for factors of 108, either pair counts.

Practice history is saved in the browser. Manage progress can export, merge, or reset it. Completed matching runs record first-try bests and fastest perfect matching times for each combination of board size, session length, and recall setting.

Chromium checks covered reopening after a miss, retiring a prompt across two dates, exporting and restoring a backup, and completing games with recall off, two, or four. The checks also exercised scoring, pause and resume, and the completion cue.

In Update 7, Sriram brought five fixes from Grok Bot’s review. The drill reserves room for older facts by capping misses at five. The app backs up unreadable progress and shows a recovery notice. Exports read the latest saved data, tabs refresh on storage changes, and an in-page reset dialog offers an export before confirmation. Completed matching runs have stable IDs for deduplication. A stress test exposed an overlapping-save race between tabs, so the app serializes progress operations where Web Locks are available.

In Update 8, Grok Bot proposed using Fog Sweep 64 misses to build the same practice history. A completed Fog Sweep round contributes first-try results to Practice weak facts. Retry counts as a miss, and a slow first-try match counts as seen. Retirement still takes two unaided correct recall answers on different days in Prime Factor 64. Both games use the same storage safeguards, and Prime Factor 64 records its own personal bests.

In Update 9, Muse proposed a local outbox to preserve a completed round when its save was waiting for another tab. The shared script stores each batch synchronously, then merges it under the existing lock. This lets the review and Project home respond immediately.

Update 10 added Easy Mode to help players build fluent recall. It groups the 64 products into Squares, Next Door, Straddle, and Split at 10 boards. Players can use a strategy reminder, replay a board without a timer, and move to Classic when ready. Classic opens by default. Easy Mode matching and recall stay in the session and leave Practice weak facts and personal bests alone.

How the name was chosen

The working name moved from Prime Match 55 to Prime Match 60 as the deck grew. At 60 facts, Sriram explicitly asked which of four names was more distinctive. ChatGPT’s recommendation at that point was Prime Match 60. The comparison separated memorability, accuracy, and how clearly each name suggested the game mechanic.

Name consideredAssessment during the discussion
Prime Match 60ChatGPT’s first choice at 60. ‘Match’ described the interaction, and the number gave the game a specific, memorable identity.
Factor MatchThe runner-up and the most literal fit for a mixed deck containing primes and composites.
Multiply MatchThe clearest about the arithmetic at a glance. ChatGPT judged it more generic.
Prime FactorChatGPT thought this could sound like an exercise in prime factorization when read on its own.

The new deck changed the choice

When the game reached 64 facts, Sriram said, “prime factor 64 has a nice ring to it. go ahead”. His preference settled the decision. The displayed title became Prime Factor 64, while the compact wordmark read Prime Factor. The number also conveyed the size of the practice pool and corresponded to the new 4-, 8-, and 16-pair layouts.

The last addition, 17 × 21 = 357, gave the deck a fourth result ending in 7, alongside 117, 187, and 247. The new result helped with the shared-ending groups and introduced the exception to the factor limit that Sriram later asked to spell out on the home page.

Other paths we considered

Some ideas were discussed and set aside before a release. Others were built, played, and then revised. The difference matters when tracing how the decisions were made.

Proposals that stayed on paper

Proposed pathDecision and reason
Build with 39 resultsThe first sketch had five boards, four of eight and a final seven. The same two-column game was first released with the larger 55-result deck and seven boards.
Stop at 45 resultsAdding just the six difficult even-by-even products would have produced a smaller expansion. The conversation moved to all qualifying composite-by-composite pairs and 55 unique results.
Deal both forms of a repeated resultSriram ruled this out when discussing the expanded deck. Each result would occupy one slot, with its eligible expressions available as alternatives across games.
Turn the forced final match into recallChatGPT proposed hiding the board when one pair remained, using that pair as the first existing recall prompt and a missed or slow pair as the second. Sriram accepted the last match by elimination and asked to group the finished cards instead.
Trade 15² for 18²This would keep the deck at 60, replace the familiar 225 with 324, and require a small ending-group adjustment. Sriram then chose to keep 15² and add 14², 16², and 18², bringing the deck to 63 before the last addition.

Shipped approaches that gave way

The free shuffle gave way to shared endings. The 15-board four-pair deal at 55 kept at least two cards per ending but left four short boards. The 14-board correction allowed one unique ending to pack the deck more tightly. At 60, the 15-board count returned with every board full.

The adjacent board-count label gave way to labels inside the dropdown, then to a separate Boards menu with selectable run lengths. The 12-pair layout yielded to the 16-pair layout after the move to 64. The game also moved from a two-check checkbox and saved setup choices to a three-option recall menu and defaults that reset on reload.
